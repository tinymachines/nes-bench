## The breakouts, mapped

Probing inside a controller socket is fiddly and a slipped tip shorts
two pins. A breakout on the breadboard is a row of holes, and a probe
clips to a link wire and stays there. So the probing moves to the
breadboard, and the four meter readings can be taken there too, with
one exception named below.

Answered 2026-09-09 from the bench notebook, photographed rather than
retyped, so the sheet and the table can be compared:

![The controller port mapping, as written at the bench](../lab/01-breakout-map-controller.jpg)

| pin | signal | NES harness | breakout lead |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | GND | brown | yellow |
| 2 | CLK | red | yellow |
| 3 | OUT0, the latch | orange | black |
| 4 | D0, the data | yellow | green |
| 5 | D3 | white | red |
| 6 | D4 | blue | not connected |
| 7 | +5 V | purple | not connected |

The NES column is step 1.1's, unchanged. Three things fall out of the
breakout column, and two of them change what happens next.

**Pins 1 and 2 are both yellow.** Ground and clock, the same colour,
adjacent, and they are exactly the two leads a probe's ground clip and
a probe's tip are about to go on. A ground clip on CLK is a short to
ground every time the console drives that line high. So this is the
first thing at the bench, before any probe touches anything:

- console **off**,
- meter on continuity,
- one lead on the board header's **brown**,
- touch each **yellow** in turn.

The one that beeps is pin 1, GND. Mark it, physically. The other is
CLK. This also does what `wiring.md`'s measure-first item 1 asked for
and step 1.1 could not: 1.1 read the pin numbers moulded into the port
housing, which is the housing's claim about itself and says nothing
about whether the harness reaches the board header unswapped. A beep
from brown to a breakout lead says it does.

**+5 V is not brought out.** So step 1.2's guard reading, purple about
5 V, cannot be taken at the breakout. It has to be taken at the port
housing or at the board header. Everything else in 1.2 can be read at
the breakout.

**That is not a rework item.** The bridge does not need the console's
+5 V. v1b powers the 74HC chain from the UNO's own 5 V, fed from the Pi
over USB: the last row of the pin table in `bench-v1b-uno.md`. One
supply feeding the chain is better than two joined at the breadboard.
What the port does still have to share is **ground**, and it does: pin
1 is on the breakout. All four lines the bridge actually touches are
there, GND, CLK, OUT0 and D0. D3 arrives as a bonus. D4 does not, and
D4 is not used.

### What the three connectors actually are

Answered by the operator 2026-09-09, and it is not what the earlier
paragraph in this file guessed. Corrected here rather than quietly:

  cable, cut in half and broken out at both ends.** They carry the same

- **The third, standalone connector is the power and reset breakout**,

So the earlier warning here, that a brown could have come off either

### What that changes about the build, and it is an improvement

`wiring.md` has the bridge tapping the console's own port harness at the
board header (J1), and borrowing the console's second port housing for
the pad (J2), with nothing cut. A controller cable cut in half gives
both of those from one sacrificed cable and touches nothing inside the
console:

- the **plug half** goes into port 1 and is the console side, which is
  what the 74HC165 answers as;
- the **pad half** has the pad on the end of it, which is what the UNO
  polls on D6, D7 and D8.

The console's own harness stays plugged into its board header, and the
second port stays free. That is worth having: the one irreversible thing
on this bench is now a cable that cost nothing, rather than a connector
inside a working console.

`wiring.md` and `bench-v1b-uno.md` still describe J1 and J2 the old way,
and the schematic still draws them that way. They are not amended yet on
purpose: the cut cable's own map is the thing being rung out right now,
and amending a sheet to match a map that has not held would put the
guess into the drawing. They get amended when step 1.1 holds with the
meter.

**Which half is which is already answered**, if the plug was in port 1
for the continuity check. A beep from the console's board header to a
breakout lead can only reach the **plug half**: the pad half is on the
far side of the cut. So the header you marked is the console side.

### The first pass was toning through the console, and it lied

The earlier table had pins 1 and 2 both on yellow. They are not. Pin 2
is blue. The first pass was rung with the cable still plugged into the
NES, and a tone through a connected console goes through the console's
own pull-ups and port buffers, so pins that share nothing beep anyway.

That is the whole two yellow scare, invented by the instrument. It is
the same failure as a stale server on a port or a `/usr/bin/node` that
is v12: the measurement was of the wrong circuit, and it was perfectly
repeatable. **Ring a cable out of circuit or do not ring it out.**

The notebook page was right about four of five and wrong about pin 2 for
the same reason. Nothing in this document guesses at a colour again.

### The published pinout does not describe this board

Two of the five conductors have no purpose yet, and it is not a small
gap. A pad runs a 4021, and the five wires it needs are GND, CLK, OUT0,
D0 and **+5 V**. Pins 2, 3 and 4 are the middle three on every published
NES pinout and nothing here disturbs them. So **pins 1 and 5 are ground
and the supply, in one order or the other**, and the published table,
which calls pin 1 ground, pin 5 D3 and pin 7 the supply, cannot be right
on this housing's numbering: pin 7 carries nothing, and a pad has no use
for D3.

The likely story is that the moulded numbers on the top row of this
housing read the other way from the reference drawing, which would make
the moulded 5 the reference's 7. That is a guess and it is not needed.
One measurement settles it.

### Do this before a probe ground clip touches anything

If pin 1 is the supply rather than ground, a probe's ground clip on it
shorts the console's 5 V rail to the scope's earth. So the order
matters:

1. Console **off and unplugged from the wall**.
2. Meter on ohms or continuity, one lead on the console's **metal
   shielding**, which is ground beyond argument.
3. Touch the **yellow** lead, then the **red** lead.
4. The one that reads near zero is **GND**. The other is **+5 V**.

Write both into the table above. Then the ground clips go on the one
that read zero, and nothing else on this bench is ambiguous again.

### Settling it, cheapest first

**1. The ohms check above**, which names ground and the supply and takes
one touch.

**2. Let the scope confirm all five.** This is the good part: the five
conductors of a controller cable identify themselves by shape, and no
numbering has to be trusted at all. Console on, a game running, the plug
back in port 1, probe grounds on the lead already marked as ground:

| what the scope shows | what the lead is |
|---|---|
| flat at 0 V | GND |
| flat at 5 V | +5 V, the supply |
| one wide pulse per frame | OUT0, the latch |
| a burst of eight narrow pulses after it | CLK |
| data between the clocks | D0 |

Five leads, four channels on this scope, and one of the five is already
known. There is no ambiguity left in that table: only one line is flat
high, only one has a single wide pulse, only one has eight.

This is what step 2.1 does anyway, and the identification falls out of
the same capture. Put the probes on, read the shapes against the table,
then leave CH1 on black and CH2 on blue and let the tool take the
measurement.

The scope is also the second opinion on the ohms check, and it is the
one that decides: whichever of yellow and red is flat at 5 V is the
supply, and the other is flat at 0 V. If those two disagree with the
meter, stop and say so, because then something is wrong that neither
instrument has named yet.

## The scope named the five, 2026-09-09

Asked of the instrument, not read off the screen. Probes at 1x, grounds
on the lead the meter called ground, console running a game:

| channel | on | vmin | vmax | mean | what it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CH1 | black | -0.04 V | 4.12 V | 0.07 V | **OUT0**, idles low, pulses high |
| CH2 | blue | 0.00 V | 5.32 V | 5.00 V | **CLK**, idles high, pulses low |
| CH4 | the non ground lead | 4.96 V | 5.08 V | 5.00 V | **+5 V**, flat |
| CH3 | composite video | -0.04 V | 1.20 V | 0.57 V | the console is running |

Three of those are decisive on their own. A line whose mean sits at its
own minimum is idling low; a line whose mean sits at its maximum is
idling high; a line with 120 mV of spread across a whole record is a
supply. No numbering was consulted and none is needed.

**So the published pinout is retired on this board.** Pin 5 is the
supply and pin 7 carries nothing, where the published table has it the
other way around. The bench table above is now the one that counts.

### Two authored claims died here

- **This document said "both OUT0 and CLK idle high".** OUT0 idles
  **low**. That was written from reasoning about a photograph of two
  flat channels, and it was wrong. The latch sits at 0 V and pulses up;
  the clock sits at 5 V and pulses down.
- **`wiring.md`'s port table** had pin 5 as D3 and pin 7 as the supply.
  Step 1.1 has always said that table is a published pinout until this
  board confirms it. It did not confirm it.

### The probe ratio was lying by a factor of ten

CH1, CH2 and CH4 were set to a **10x** ratio with **1x** probes on them,
so the screen read a 5 V rail as 50 V and a logic swing as 56 V. Nothing
was damaged and nothing was wrong with the bench: the scope was
multiplying by ten because it had been told to.

`headd.py`'s `arm()` sends `:CHANnel<n>:PROBe 1` on every channel it
arms, so the tool was never going to be fooled by this and step 2.1's
numbers would have been right either way. **A person reading the screen
would have been fooled completely.** That is the useful half: the
instrument's own configuration is a claim like any other, and the first
question of any reading that looks an order of magnitude wrong is
whether the scope was told the truth about what is plugged into it.

## Step B, the tools: step 2.1

This is the one that matters. It arms both channels, waits for a latch

- the latch pulse width,
- the clock pulse width,
- the gap between clocks,
- the polls per second.

Those four are marked **authored** across `docs/wiring.md` and the

## Step 2.1 holds, and it found something better than four numbers

Two records were taken, twenty minutes apart, 12 million points per
channel at 50 MS/s, 240 ms each. **Both are internally rock steady and
they disagree with each other**, which is the finding.

| | authored | A, 21:05 | B, 21:14, pad in | C, 21:30, pad out |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| latch high | "a few us" | 4.44 us | **3.32 us** | **3.32 us** |
| clock low | "a few hundred ns" | 0.62 us | 0.600 us | 0.580 us |
| between clocks | "~7 us" | 15.64 us | **10.62 us** | **10.62 us** |
| polls per second | "60" | 60.0997 | 60.0997 | 60.0997 |
| clocks per poll | 8 | 8 on all 14 | 8 on all 14 | 8 on all 14 |

Steady inside each record: B's latch measured 3.32 us on fifteen
consecutive polls and its 105 clock gaps ran 10.60 to 10.62 us. A was
the same kind of steady at its own values. This is not noise and none of
them is wrong.

**B and C are two independent captures that agree to the sample.** The
0.02 us of difference in the clock low is exactly one sample at 50 MS/s.
So the measurement repeats, which is what makes A's disagreement worth
taking seriously rather than explaining away.

### The gamepad was not the variable

C was taken with the gamepad unplugged, to test the obvious guess about
what changed between A and B. **It changed nothing**: same latch, same
clock period, same poll rate, to one sample. A pad only answers on D0
and this step does not look at D0, so in hindsight it could not have
mattered, but the guess was cheap to kill and killing it is worth as
much as confirming it would have been.

What actually differed between A and B is therefore still open, and it
is open in a way that cannot be closed now: **A's record was overwritten
and nobody had written down what was on the screen.** Step 2.1 now asks,
and stores the answer beside the numbers, so the next pair can be
compared.

### The port's timing is the game's, in whole CPU cycles

The 2A03 runs at 21477272/12 = 1789772.7 Hz, one cycle every 0.5587 us.
Divide the measurements by it:

| measurement | cycles | to the nearest whole |
|---|---|---|
| latch high, run B, 3.32 us | 5.942 | **6** (0.97 percent) |
| latch high, run A, 4.44 us | 7.947 | **8** (0.67 percent) |
| clock low, both, 0.6 us | 1.07 | **1** |
| between clocks, run B, 10.62 us | 19.007 | **19** (0.04 percent) |
| between clocks, run A, 15.64 us | 27.992 | **28** (0.03 percent) |

Every one lands on a whole cycle, and the two clock periods land to
better than half a part in a thousand. Nothing was fitted to make that
happen; the cycle count is the console's own clock, derived from the
crystal, and the microseconds came off the scope.

So **the latch width and the clock spacing are not properties of the
console at all. They are cycle counts in whatever code is running.** The
latch is high for the gap between the game's two writes to $4016, six
cycles in one routine and eight in another. Each clock is one read of
$4016, one cycle low, and the spacing is the game's read loop: 19 cycles
in one, 28 in the other. The console was running different code at 21:05
and 21:14.

The two numbers that did NOT move are the two that are structural: eight
clocks per poll, and one poll per frame.

### What that does to the authored numbers

The authored "~7 us between clocks" was not wrong so much as
unqualified. Seven microseconds is about 12 cycles, which is a tight
unrolled read, and tight unrolled reads exist. This console with this
cartridge does 19 and 28. **The bridge cannot be designed to any one of
them.** The 165 has to have its bit up before the fastest poll any game
might do, not before the one that happened to be on screen.

That is a better answer than the four numbers the step set out to get,
and it is the sort of answer a single capture would have hidden. It also
means step 2.1 wants running more than once, with the game state written
down beside it, and the bound that matters is the **shortest** clock
period ever seen rather than the median of one record.

**B and C are what the log carries**, because those are the runs the
tool took. A is quoted here from its own record, which the tool refused
at the time for a reason that turned out to be its own bug.

The step now also records the cycle counts beside the microseconds, so
the notebook carries the number that means something rather than only
the number the scope measured.

### The tool refused the first record, twice over

Both refusals were the tool's fault. Both are fixed, and
`tools/check-bringup.py` puts each back to red under mutation.

- **"the latch channel is flat (76 to 77 of 255)"** on a perfect
  record. The level test thresholded on the 2nd and 98th percentiles,
  which assumes a line spends a couple of percent of its time on each
  level. This console's latch is high for **0.027 percent** of the
  record and its clock is low for 0.031 percent. Both percentiles sat
  inside the same level, the test saw one code of range out of 255, and
  it blamed the probe. `levels()` now splits at the midpoint of the
  extremes and takes the median of each side, which no duty cycle can
  fool, and a separate sample count still catches a dead probe and a
  lone spike.
- **`clock_period_us` came back as 782,000,000.** `np.diff` over an
  edge index array is in samples, and the microsecond scaling was
  applied without dividing by the sample rate. Every other line in that
  function divides first. Nothing had ever run it on a real record.

The same lesson twice: **the first real measurement is the first test of
the measuring code.** A statistic chosen for a plausible signal is an
assumption about the signal, and a needle is not a flat line.
